Resource Allocation in Different Market Systems

Unit 1.4 in Chapter 1: Basic economic ideas and resources

ASSESS WHETHER ALL MARKET ECONOMIES SHOULD BECOME MIXED ECONOMIES.

Model Answer written by Bryan Yeo:

AO1 (2 MARKS)

A market economy is one in which market forces are allowed to guide the allocation of resources within a society. A mixed economy is an economy that has the features of both a market economy and a centrally planned economy, using the forces of supply and demand to allocate resources, with the government sometimes intervening to influence the allocation of resources, usually to address market and information failures.

AO2 (6 MARKS)
Must be two-sided; the first paragraph is an ideal structure with two main points: the price mechanism and consumer sovereignty

In a pure market economy, there is no (or minimal) government intervention; only market forces in the form of supply and demand influence the allocation of resources. Hence, this enables price to play a key role, acting as a reliable signal for decision-making when there is a change in consumer preferences. For instance, a producer would seek to reduce prices when faced with a decrease in demand. As a result, this allows them to efficiently allocate resources towards the production of goods and services that satisfy the wants of consumers. Furthermore, minimal government intervention spurs competition amongst producers. This is because low barriers to entry and the profit-maximising incentive drive more and more firms to enter the market and, as such, compete to produce better goods and services to earn more revenue. This incentivises firms to cut down on their costs of production to maximise profit as they seek to beat out competitors, hence making production more efficient. It also encourages firms to innovate as they seek to raise the quality of their goods and lower the price of their goods, attracting more consumers and capturing a larger share of the market. As a result, consumers experience consumer sovereignty as they have a wide choice of products to choose from when more firms enter the market.

However, it is important to note that no public goods (goods that are non-rival and non-excludable in nature) will be provided in this market structure. This is due to the free-rider problem, where consumers can enjoy goods without paying for them. The problem of information failure can also cause firms to overproduce demerit goods and underproduce merit goods as price does not reflect the externalities generated by these types of goods. For example, merit goods such as education and healthcare, when left to market forces, may be underconsumed as some are unable to afford it due to its overpriced nature and some may be unwilling to pay for them as they are not fully aware of the positive externalities generated by these goods, leading to inequitable access to education and healthcare, whereas demerit goods such as cigarettes are overconsumed due to them being underpriced and overvalued as consumers do not fully understand the negative externalities associated with the consumption of these demerit goods. There may also exist some markets that are prone to price fluctuations if left fully to market, where prices remain unstable (too high or too low) for long periods of time, which may affect consumer and business confidence.

AO3 (4 MARKS)
May consider public goods, merit goods, demerit goods, and price mechanism, leading to an overall conclusion (last paragraph)

While a free market economy has its advantages, such as its more efficient allocation of resources and its promotion of competition and incentive to innovate, the absence of the provision of public goods and the overproduction and underproduction of demerit and merit goods, respectively, may outweigh these advantages in the long run and may end up causing market failures and deadweight losses, both of which result in the loss of economic efficiency.

A mixed economy allows social welfare to be protected while simultaneously ensuring the efficient allocation of goods and services, albeit less efficient than that of a free market economy. The transition from a free market to a mixed economy would mean the government plays an important role in providing merit goods and public goods, ensuring more consumers are able to access merit goods and necessities such as education and healthcare, ensuring a more equitable distribution of merit goods. They are also able to play an important role in regulating the consumption of demerit goods and addressing information failure, making people more aware of the dangers of consuming demerit goods excessively and reducing the consumption of demerit goods by introducing regulations and taxes, thus reducing the negative externalities generated by demerit goods. They will also be able to intervene when markets encounter unstable prices, introducing regulations to stabilise prices, such as through the use of minimum or maximum pricing mechanisms. This environment also still allows firms to compete against each other, as price still acts as a reliable signal of changes in consumer preferences, which still allows the efficient allocation of resources.

However, there should not be full or high government intervention as this may distort prices and make firms more inefficient in the long run as it could stagnate competition, leading to a drop in efficiency and innovation.

Therefore, a transition from a free market to a mixed economy may be warranted, as the limitations of free markets, such as the absence of provision of public goods, can be addressed through implementing this market structure. The government can intervene to address market failures in free markets, allowing both societal welfare to be ensured and encouraging efficiency, but must make sure their level of intervention is optimal to carefully balance both economic efficiency and societal well-being. In conclusion, all market economies should transition to a mixed economy.

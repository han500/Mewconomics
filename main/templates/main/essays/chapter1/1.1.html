AO1 (4 MARKS)
Introduce the PPC

A production possibility curve (PPC) is a graph that shows the maximum possible combinations of two goods that can be produced in an economy when all of its resources are fully utilised, given that its state of technology is fixed. It can be used as an indicator of scarcity. It can also be used to indicate choice and opportunity cost as an economy moves along the PPC with different combinations of goods and services chosen.

AO2 (4 MARKS)
Using the PPC to explain scarcity [2], choice [2] and opportunity cost [2]

Scarcity is the central economic problem, where there are not enough resources to satisfy the needs and wants of all. In a PPC, this is illustrated by point C, a point of production that is unattainable due to the limited amount of resources an economy has. Opportunity cost is the next best alternative forgone, and using the PPC diagram above, we can see that its concave-like shape is indicative of the increasing opportunity costs incurred along the curve when shifting more resources to produce more of another good, as resources used to produce one good may not be especially suitable to produce another good, which causes opportunity cost to increase when producing more of another good. In this case, the opportunity cost of increasing production of Good X from Q2 to Q3 is Q0-Q1 units of Good Y, indicated by a movement along the PPC from point A to point B. Last but not least, choice is made necessary due to scarcity and can be shown on the PPC through a movement along the PPC. When an economy decides to produce more of a good, it must sacrifice production of a few units of another good. For example, the movement along the PPC from point A to B shows that although more of Good X is produced, units of Good Y are sacrificed (less units of Good B are produced) to enable this.

AO3 (2 MARKS)

Although the PPC serves as an effective tool to illustrate the concepts of scarcity, choice, and opportunity cost, it rests on several key assumptions that are unrealistic. Firstly, it only measures the relationship between two goods, which oversimplifies complex economies where multiple goods and services are sold. It also does not take into account external factors such as market imperfections and changes in demand, which may drastically affect the opportunity costs of a good and the amount of goods that can be produced within an economy. It also assumes that technological states remain static, which is often not the case, and technological advancements in the long run may alter the production possibilities of an economy, again changing the opportunity costs of producing a good and choices made due to the higher number of goods that can be produced.

In conclusion, scarcity can be illustrated on the PPC by drawing a point outside its frontier; opportunity cost and choice can be illustrated by showing a movement along the curve, showing that in order for more units of a good to be produced, units of another good must be given up for this to happen. And although PPC serves as a simple and effective visual aid to illustrate unattainable points and trade-offs along the curve, the key assumptions it rests on are often not applicable in the real world, which may affect the reliability of a PPC in showing opportunity cost and production possibilities.

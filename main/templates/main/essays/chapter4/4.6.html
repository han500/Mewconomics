Price Stability

Unit 4.6 in Chapter 4: The Macroeconomy

DISCUSS WHETHER IT IS THE BEHAVIOUR OF PRODUCERS, CONSUMERS OR GOVERNMENTS THAT IT IS MOST LIKELY TO CAUSE INFLATION.[12]

SOURCE: CAIE, ECONOMICS, 9708, PAPER 2, VARIANT 2, MAY / JUNE, 2011

Model Answer written by Yijun:

AO1 (2 MARKS)

Inflation is defined as a sustained increase in the general price level in an economy over a period of time, usually three quarters. The annual rate of inflation is measured by the percentage change in the level of consumer prices. Through analysis, economists can differentiate between demand-pull and cost-push inflation and respond accordingly.

AO2 (6 MARKS)

Demand-pull inflation results from the growth of aggregate demand (AD) being allowed to exceed the relative growth of what the supply-side capacity can meet. The result is excess demand for goods and services and pressure on businesses to raise prices. Just how much firms raise their prices depends on how much their costs rise as a result of increasing output. An increase in consumer demand, for instance, due to expectations of higher prices in the future, is a key component of increased prices. Governments may contribute to inflation with a fiscal stimulus such as a cut in direct or indirect taxation or increasing government spending by borrowing from the banking system, which feeds through extra demand in the circular flow of the economy. However, fiscal policies that encourage firm investment will lead to a rise in AD due to increased investment, which increases capacity to absorb all demand and may have downward pressures on inflation

We must also acknowledge cost-push inflation. Such shifts occur when costs of production rise independently of demand, and can be divided into push-profit and tax-push inflation. If firms face a rise in costs, they will respond partly by raising prices and passing costs on to consumers. Push-profit inflation is derived from producers. This is where firms abuse their power as monopolies to generate greater profits by pushing prices up independently of demand. For example, the decisions of the major utility companies providing electricity, gas, or water are based on their prices for the year ahead. From governments, tax-push inflation is when taxation adds to the cost of production. For example, a rise in indirect taxes such as VAT. Additionally, import-push inflation occurs when the government devalues its currency or allows foreign exchange to depreciate as part of its exchange rate policy. This causes the prices of imported raw materials to rise and, hence, the cost of production to rise.

AO3 (4 MARKS)

Overall, it is essential to identify the causes of inflation in order to enact effective policies that address the correct causes. Through the analysis of the two main causes of inflation, the contribution to inflation from different stakeholders, such as consumers, producers, and the government, varies. Demand-pull inflation is significantly affected by consumer demand and their future expectations of the economy. This is highly volatile and depends on the current market conditions. Governments have multiple ways to influence these expectations and demand-pull influences with policies. On the other hand, cost-push inflation is, to a greater extent, dependent on producer behaviour rather than that of consumers. Even in this scenario, governments are observed to have influence, namely through policies. In conclusion, the empirical evidence suggests it is largely the behaviour of the government. This creates a stronger case for policymakers to tune into the causes of inflation at all times.

WITH THE HELP OF AN AD/AS DIAGRAM(S), EXPLAIN COST-PUSH INFLATION AND DEMAND-PULL INFLATION IN AN ECONOMY AND CONSIDER IN WHAT CIRCUMSTANCES ONE MAY BE MORE DAMAGING THAN THE OTHER.[8]

Model Answer written by Hilary Yong Mun Jie:

AO1 (2 MARKS)

Inflation is the sustained rise in general price levels of goods and services in an economy over a given period of time.

Cost-push inflation is defined as inflation caused by a rise in costs of production for goods and services in an economy. When there is a general increase in costs of production in an economy - for example, let’s assume that there is a tax levied on transport systems - aggregate supply decreases as costs rise, and this is demonstrated in the graph as AS shifts left to AS1, thus facilitating an increase in general price levels from P to P1, assuming aggregate demand stays constant.

Demand-pull inflation is defined as inflation caused by an increase in aggregate demand. This could be caused by a myriad of factors - for instance, a rise in the minimum wage could cause aggregate demand to increase as more people would have increased disposable income. This is shown by a rightwards shift of the aggregate demand curve from AD to AD1, thereby increasing the general price levels from P to P1.

AO2 (4 MARKS)

In the case where cost-push inflation could be more damaging than demand-pull inflation, it could be when the increase in production costs severely disrupts supply chains, leading to widespread shortages. For example, if a natural disaster or geopolitical conflict causes a sudden spike in oil prices, industries reliant on fossil fuels face increased costs that are often passed on to consumers. This type of inflation can lead to stagflation—where rising prices coincide with stagnant economic growth and high unemployment—creating a particularly challenging economic environment. Consumers may reduce their spending due to higher prices, exacerbating economic stagnation, while businesses may struggle to maintain profitability and employment levels.

On the other hand, demand-pull inflation can be more damaging when it results from excessive consumer spending driven by an overheated economy, such as during a rapid recovery from a recession. In this scenario, demand outpaces supply, leading to significant price increases that can erode purchasing power. If the inflation persists, it can lead to tighter monetary policies, higher interest rates, and reduced investment, ultimately slowing down economic growth. The impact is particularly harmful if wages do not keep pace with rising prices, leading to decreased consumer confidence and potential economic downturns. This cycle can create a volatile environment where inflation expectations rise, making it harder to stabilise the economy.

AO3 (2 MARKS)

In evaluation, it is essential to consider the broader economic context and the underlying causes of each type. Cost-push inflation often leads to supply-side constraints that can trigger stagflation, a scenario detrimental to economic growth and employment, especially in developing economies heavily reliant on imports for essential goods. Conversely, demand-pull inflation may indicate a thriving economy; however, if unchecked, it can spiral into sustained inflationary pressures that erode consumer purchasing power and destabilise markets. Additionally, the effectiveness of policy responses, such as monetary tightening for demand-pull inflation or supply-side interventions for cost-push inflation, can vary significantly, influencing the long-term economic landscape. Therefore, the assessment of which inflation type is more damaging depends on specific economic conditions, the flexibility of labour markets, and the responsiveness of fiscal and monetary policies.

Aggregate Demand and Aggregate Supply Analysis

Unit 4.3 in Chapter 4: The Macroeconomy

DISCUSS THE CAUSES OF AN INCREASE IN AGGREGATE DEMAND. ASSESS WHETHER SUCH AN INCREASE WILL ALWAYS CAUSE INFLATION. USE DIAGRAMS TO SUPPORT YOUR ANSWER.

Model Answer written by Anuj:

AO1 (2 MARKS)

Aggregate demand (AD) is the total demand for goods and services in an economy at a given price level, comprising consumption (C), investment (I), government spending (G), and net exports (X-M).

AO2 (6 MARKS)

An increase in AD can be caused by various factors:

Firstly, there could be an increase in consumption. This may occur due to lower interest rates which makes borrowing cheaper and saving less attractive, encouraging consumer spending, especially on durables such as cars. Consumption may also increase due to higher disposable incomes due to income tax cuts, for example.

Secondly, there could be an increase in investment, perhaps due to lower corporate taxes that encourage reinvestment of profits, increasing AD.

Additionally, governments may increase spending on infrastructure, education or healthcare which directly boost AD. For instance, building new roads creates jobs and raises overall demand in the economy.

Lastly, net exports may improve if there is a depreciation of the country’s currency which would make exports cheaper and more desirable, increasing AD.

AO3 (4 MARKS)

The impact of an increase in AD on inflation depends on the position of the economy relative to its productive capacity, represented by the aggregate supply (AS) curve.

In Keynesian economics, there may be inflation in the short run if the economy is operating at near full capacity. The upward-sloping section of the AS curve illustrates this, where higher demand pushes prices up. However, if there is sufficient spare capacity, an increase in AD may result in higher output without inflation. The horizontal section of the Keynesian AS curve represents this. However, according to classical economists, in the long run, the economy operates at full employment, and any increase in AD beyond this point leads to inflation without a rise in output. This is shown by the vertical LRAS curve.

Therefore, an increase in AD does not always cause inflation. The extent of inflation depends on factors such as the level of spare capacity, the responsiveness of AS, and whether supply-side policies accompany demand-side changes.
